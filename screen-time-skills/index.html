Dear Mummy,

Let me describe a moment you might recognise.

You are scrolling through your phone after a long day. A video catches your attention. Maybe it is a teenage boy showing an app he helped build. Maybe it is a girl explaining how she edited a beautiful little film. Or a young person designing something you would have expected from a professional.

You pause. You watch it again.

“Ah. How did this child learn to do all this?”

There is a little surprise in that question. Perhaps admiration. Perhaps curiosity. And, somewhere underneath, another thought you might not say aloud:

“Could my own child learn something like that too?”

Then you look across the room. Your own child also has a phone. They know how to find a game in seconds. They know which videos are trending before you do. They can spend an hour moving from one clip to another without looking up.

You love your child. You know they have their own gifts. You are not trying to make them compete with a stranger's son or daughter.

Still, you wonder what could happen if some of those hours were used differently.

What if they learned to edit a short video instead of only watching other people's edits? What if they loved football and started learning how to explain a match clearly? What if the child who spends time drawing could make their first digital illustration?

You do not need your child to become a famous young entrepreneur. You want to know that, while the world is changing, your child is learning how to do something with the tools already around them.

And you want to encourage that without inviting more phone addiction into your home.

That second part matters. Because it is possible for a child to learn a useful skill and still be spending far too much time on a screen. We are going to talk honestly about both sides.

THE QUESTION BEHIND THE QUESTION

What Do Those Children Know That Mine Has Not Discovered Yet?

When you see a child doing something impressive online, the finished result is usually all you see.

You do not see the first terrible attempt. The confusing tutorial. The adult who introduced them to the tool. The Saturday afternoon they spent trying to understand it. The small mistakes nobody posted.

It can look like that child was simply born knowing what to do.

Some children have unusual talents, of course. But a head start is not always about being smarter. Sometimes somebody showed the child what was possible. Sometimes they had permission to try. Sometimes an older person took an interest long enough for the first small attempt to become a second one.

The difference is not always intelligence or money. Sometimes, it is exposure.

And that is hopeful news for a parent.

It means you do not have to stand over your child and demand, “Why are you not like that boy?” That question may only make them feel inadequate.

You can ask a much better one: “What does my own child enjoy, and how can I help them discover the useful skills connected to it?”

It is a smaller question. It is also one you can begin answering this week.

A REAL EXAMPLE FROM MY FAMILY

I Have Seen What Early Exposure Can Do

Growing up, I heard a familiar sentence at home: “Go and read your book.”

My father is a baker. He worked hard to provide for us. He was not a technology person, so to him, a child spending time on a screen could easily look like a child wasting time.

I understand where he was coming from. He cared about education. He did not want his children to lose their future to distraction.

But I also have an uncle who is a software developer. He approached technology differently with his own children.

His two sons were exposed to computers and software early. They still had ordinary teenage interests. They played games. They were not expected to spend every free moment working.

But they were also shown that technology is not just somewhere to watch and consume. It can be somewhere to make things.

Those two boys went on to build an app with real users while they were still teenagers. At one point, they were celebrating reaching 200 users.

Think about that: other people were using something they had helped make.

I am a software developer myself. Yet I started much later than those boys. Their early exposure gave them the chance to practise things I had not even discovered at their age.

I am telling you this because I know what I am talking about when I say that an introduction at the right time can change what a child believes is possible.

But please notice what I am not saying. I am not promising your child will build an app, reach 200 users or earn money. Those boys had their own circumstances, interests and support. Their experience is an example, not a guaranteed result.

My point is simpler: if nobody ever shows a child what they might create, how are they supposed to know where to begin?

And the answer does not have to be coding. That is just one example from my family.

ONE DEVICE, DIFFERENT POSSIBILITIES

Two Children Can Hold The Same Phone And Come Away With Different Things

Imagine two children each spending ninety minutes on a phone.

CHILD AWatches random clips. Opens another app. Keeps scrolling. When the time ends, there is little they can explain beyond what they watched.
CHILD BWatches a short lesson, tries a small task, gets stuck, tries again and finishes a simple drawing, edit, explanation or written piece.

The phone is the same kind of tool. The hours are similar. But the child who practised something has a small result they can point to.

That does not make Child B better than Child A. Children need rest, play, stories, laughter and time to do absolutely nothing productive. Not every minute needs to become a project.

The real problem is when entertainment becomes the only thing the child knows how to do with the device—and when they cannot stop, even to sleep, study or spend time with the people around them.

There is a difference between enjoying a screen, building with a screen and consuming without control.

The guide helps you recognise those differences. Then it helps you find a healthier balance for your own household.

Because the aim is not to take a child who scrolls for four hours and turn them into a child who studies coding for four hours. The aim is to reduce uncontrolled screen time, protect what matters and give some of the remaining time a more useful direction.

WHERE YOUR CHILD CAN START

Your Child Already Has Clues. You Just Need To Notice Them.

Start with what they naturally talk about.

The child who talks about football all weekend might enjoy learning to record a short match analysis. The one who loves clothes might enjoy sketching fashion ideas. The one who makes up stories might enjoy writing dialogue or recording a simple audio story.

The child who asks how every video effect works might enjoy trying basic editing. Another child may be interested in animation, cooking, photography, music, maths or design.

And one child may not be interested in making anything digital at all. That is fine too.

Try this conversation tonight

Instead of asking, “Why are you always on that phone?” try:

“What is one thing you enjoy watching or doing online?”

Listen. Then ask:

“Would you like to try making something small connected to that?”

Do not give them your answer. Let them show you what interests them.

There is no need to announce a big new family programme. You do not have to buy a laptop before this conversation. You do not have to know how to edit videos or write code yourself.

You are simply opening a door.

Maybe your child walks through it immediately. Maybe they need a few attempts before they find something they like. The point is to make discovery possible, without turning their childhood into another competition.

AN IMPORTANT WARNING

But “My Child Is Learning” Is Not Permission For Endless Screen Time

This is where some well-meaning parents can make another mistake.

They discover that a child can learn something useful online, so they stop worrying about how long the child stays there. But useful screen time can still be excessive.

Imagine your daughter spends two hours practising video editing. She produces something real. You are proud of her.

Then you discover her homework is unfinished. She skipped a family meal. She slept too late and struggled to wake up for school.

Was the editing useful? Yes. Should that routine continue unchanged? No.

A good skill does not cancel the need for sleep, schoolwork, movement or family connection.

So we do not just ask, “Is the screen time productive?” We also ask, “What is it costing?”

This is why the book includes practical routines, age-appropriate boundaries, a family agreement and a weekly planner. You can make room for learning without handing the entire evening over to the phone.

If your child needs one hour to complete schoolwork, that hour stays protected. If a useful project can fit into thirty minutes after assignments, give it that space. If bedtime is being affected, the screen has to stop—even if the child says they are learning.

And if the device has become a substitute for real conversations, shared meals or time with siblings, that needs attention too.

We are not trying to raise a child who is impressive on the internet but disconnected from real life.

We want a child who can use technology well and live well without it.

YOU DO NOT NEED TO BE THE EXPERT

“But I Don't Know Anything About Coding Or Editing.”

Good. You do not need to.

A parent is not required to become a programming teacher just because a child is curious about technology. Your job is to guide the environment around the child.

You can notice what they enjoy, ask what they are trying, help them find a sensible place to begin and agree on when the screen must go off.

Think about how you might support a child who loves football. You may never have played professionally, but you can ask them what position they enjoy, watch a match together, encourage practice and make sure football does not replace school.

Technology can be approached in the same spirit.

Ask your child to explain one new thing in plain language. Ask them to show you a small result. You can recognise effort and honesty even when you cannot judge every technical detail.

A better question than “How many hours were you online?”

“What did you learn? Can you explain it? Can you try it yourself? Can you show me what you made?”

You are encouraging understanding, not just time spent in front of a screen.

And if your child needs help beyond what you know, that does not make you a bad parent. It simply means the next step may involve a suitable teacher, a free beginner lesson or someone you trust who understands that interest.

The most important thing is that your child is not trying to figure out every choice alone.

WHAT CHANGE CAN LOOK LIKE

Imagine A Different Conversation In Your Home

Instead of hearing, “Mummy, just five more minutes,” every evening, imagine occasionally hearing:

“Mummy, come and see what I made.”

It might be a simple drawing. A rough video edit. A written story. A small school presentation they can explain without reading every word from the screen.

Perhaps the first attempt is not very good. That is fine. You are not waiting for a masterpiece.

You are looking for a child who begins to understand that the screen can be a tool, not just a place where other people's work passes by.

And imagine being able to say, when the time is up, “I'm glad you enjoyed making that. Now put the phone down. Homework and sleep still matter.”

That is the balance we are working towards.

This week

Notice one interest, have one calm conversation, choose one small activity and agree on a simple boundary that protects the rest of the day.

Over the next few weeks

Help your child explain what they are learning, show progress and adjust the schedule where it is not working. Keep room for ordinary play and family life.

Over time

Work towards better judgment, growing independence and an ability to try new things. The pace will differ from child to child; these are goals, not guaranteed results.

You do not have to wait for your child to become a teenager before introducing healthy digital habits. And if they already are a teenager, you can still begin with a conversation that respects their growing independence.

A SMALL START YOU CAN TRY

You Do Not Need A Big Project. You Need A First One.

Suppose your child loves watching football highlights.

You might be tempted to find an expensive course and announce that they must now become a sports analyst. Please don't.

Start with something tiny: ask them to choose a match they remember and explain three moments that changed it. They can write a paragraph, record a short explanation or make a very simple presentation if the tools are already available.

If they enjoy that, the next step might be learning how to arrange clips or improve the explanation. If they do not, you have learned something about their interests without losing money or turning the week into an argument.

The important difference is moving from “I watched it” towards “I tried to explain, apply or make something.”

In the book, I encourage you to look for what the child can learn, explain, apply and show. It gives you something more useful to discuss than screen time alone.

QUESTIONS YOU MAY BE ASKING

Before You Decide, Let Me Answer What Might Be On Your Mind

You are not buying the promise of a brilliant child by next Monday. You are deciding whether you want a clearer way to guide the child you already know and love. These are fair questions to ask first.

1. Is this book going to make me compare my child to other children?

No. Seeing a child do something remarkable can open your eyes to a possibility, but comparison should never become the way you speak to your own child. The guide encourages you to start from your child's interests, age and circumstances. The question is not “Why aren't you like that child?” It is “What might you enjoy learning, and how can I help you try?”

2. Does my child have to learn coding or become a content creator?

Not at all. Coding and video editing are examples, not requirements. Useful digital habits can include research, writing, design, presentations, problem-solving or learning to check whether information is trustworthy. Some children may prefer activities away from screens. The goal is intentional use, healthy boundaries and opportunities to explore—not forcing every child into the same career.

3. My child is interested only in games and short videos. Where do I begin?

Start by getting curious instead of immediately dismissing that interest. What makes the game enjoyable? What does the child notice in the videos? An interest can lead to a small conversation about storytelling, drawing, explaining rules or how a video is put together. You can also set reasonable limits. The child does not have to turn every hobby into a lesson.

4. What if my child says, “I don't want to create anything”?

Then listen. Your child is a person, not a project. You can invite them to try something small later, but pressure often makes children less willing to explore. The screen-time routines, family agreement and screen-free activities can still help even when digital creation is not their interest. A healthier home is already a worthwhile outcome.

5. Won't learning digital skills increase the time my child spends on the phone?

It could, if there are no limits. That is exactly why this guide does not treat “educational” as a free pass. If a child spends four hours learning but loses sleep, misses homework or stops talking to the family, something is wrong. The book and planners help you protect those essentials first, then fit any useful screen activity into the time left.

6. I don't know how to code, edit or use most of these apps myself. Can I still help?

Yes. You can help a child choose one manageable task, check whether it is age-appropriate, ask them to explain what they learned and decide when the device goes off. You do not need to know every technical answer. The guide is written for parents, not professional developers. It focuses on the decisions and conversations you can make at home.

7. Do I need to buy my child a laptop or expensive software?

No expensive purchase is required to begin applying the parenting guidance. Start with the devices and resources you already have, and choose activities that fit your household. Not every example will suit every device or child. You can also start with offline planning, writing, drawing and discussion before deciding whether a digital tool is necessary.

8. What about costly data and unreliable electricity in Nigeria?

Those are real limitations. You do not need constant streaming or daily downloads to begin changing how your family approaches screens. Short, planned periods of online use can be paired with offline practice. The bundle also includes inexpensive or free screen-free activity ideas. Adjust the routine to what your home can actually sustain.

9. We share a phone at home, and I have more than one child. Will this still apply?

Yes, the general principles can be adapted. Agree on turns, use age-appropriate boundaries and make space for activities that do not depend on a device. A younger child and a teenager may need different limits. The family agreement and weekly planner give you a place to begin those discussions without pretending all siblings are the same.

10. My children are different ages. Is there guidance for each of them?

The eBook covers ages 5–7, 8–11 and 12–16. A younger child needs more direct supervision and simple routines; an older child needs more explanation, responsibility and respect. You can use the same overall approach in one household while adjusting the expectations to the child in front of you.

11. My teenager already argues whenever I mention screen time. Is it too late?

It is not too late to start a different conversation, although trust may take time to rebuild. Begin by listening to what the phone means to them. Explain the concerns—sleep, homework, privacy, family time—without humiliating them. Then agree on a small boundary you can enforce consistently. This is not a magic script, but it is a more constructive starting point than another shouting match.

12. What if the main problem is that we hardly spend time together as a family?

Then connection deserves attention alongside device rules. Children need parents who are emotionally available, not only materially supportive. The Screen-Free Activity Planner and 50 family activities are there to help parents and siblings spend more meaningful time together. Sometimes the most useful change is not another app; it is a conversation, a shared task or an evening with phones put away.

13. What about online strangers, unsafe content and privacy?

Creating something online should never require a child to share private details, interact with strangers or open accounts without appropriate supervision. Keep safety and age requirements in mind when choosing activities. The guide encourages active parental involvement and boundaries. You can practise many early skills privately, or offline, without publishing anything.

14. How soon should I expect to see a change?

Every family is different. The 7-Day Reset gives you a manageable way to begin, not a guarantee that an established habit will disappear in a week. Early progress may be a calmer conversation, one completed routine or a child showing interest in a small project. Lasting changes usually take patience, consistency and adjustments along the way.
